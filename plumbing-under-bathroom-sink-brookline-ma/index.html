What's Under a Bathroom Sink in a Brookline, MA Home

Open the vanity door and you will find five or six parts. Each one fails in its own way, and most leaks start at a joint you can reach.

By the Brookline 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 23, 2026 · Updated October 23, 2026

Call (617) 752-0573

Under a bathroom sink you will find two supply lines with shutoff valves (called stops), a drain tailpiece, a curved P-trap, a wall pipe called the trap arm, and on most sinks a pop-up rod linking the stopper to the faucet lever. That is the whole system. Water goes in through the stops and out through the trap.

Most problems are a dripping joint, a slow drain or a stopper that will not hold water. You can handle several of these with a bucket and a pair of pliers. A few, like a corroded stop that will not turn or a wet wall behind the trap arm, need a plumber. If your faucet itself is the problem, see our faucet repair service. We run the same checks in older flats across Allston and Jamaica Plain.

The parts

Reading the cabinet from the wall to the floor

Start at the wall and work down. Each part has one job.

  1. Supply stops. Two small valves on the wall or floor, hot on the left, cold on the right. A quarter-turn or multi-turn handle shuts water to this sink only.
  2. Supply lines. Braided stainless or chrome-plated tubes between each stop and the faucet. They have a rubber or fiber washer inside the nut.
  3. Pop-up assembly. A pivot rod through the back of the drain body, lifted by a strap with several holes. It raises and lowers the stopper.
  4. Tailpiece. The short vertical pipe under the sink drain flange, usually 1 1/4 inch.
  5. P-trap. The U-shaped bend that holds a plug of water and blocks sewer gas. Slip nuts and nylon washers hold it together.
  6. Trap arm and wall stub. The horizontal pipe into the wall. A drain vent behind it lets air in so the water leaves smoothly.

Common problems

Leaks and drips: where to look first

Dry the cabinet floor, put a paper towel under each joint, and run the tap for a minute. The wet spot shows you the source.

  • ✓Drip from a slip nut. Loose nut or a flattened nylon washer. Hand-tighten, then a quarter-turn with pliers.
  • ✓Drip at the stop. The packing nut behind the handle is weeping. Snug it, or the stop needs replacement.
  • ✓Water near the pop-up rod. The ball seat or gasket at the back of the drain body has worn.
  • ✓Slow draining with a gurgle. Hair and soap build up in the trap and tailpiece. Clean it by hand.
  • ✓Cabinet smells like sewer. A dry trap, a cracked trap or a missing seal at the wall.
  • ✓Rust bloom on a stop or a chalky green crust on brass. The stop is corroding and will likely seize or leak when turned.
Safe homeowner work

What you can do, and where to stop

Clearing a clogged trap and snugging a slip nut are routine homeowner tasks. Put a bucket under the trap, unscrew the two slip nuts, pull the trap, rinse it, and reassemble with the washers facing the right way. Test with water running for a full minute.

Stop if a stop valve spins freely without cutting off water, if the wall pipe leaks, if you see corroded galvanized pipe, or if the vanity floor is soft. Forcing a corroded stop often snaps the stem and floods the room.

A step-by-step

Cleaning a bathroom sink trap

Set a bucket under the trap. Hand-loosen the slip nut above and below the U-bend (if they resist, use channel-lock pliers wrapped in a rag to protect the finish). Slide the trap out. Expect black sludge and often a hair mat.

Rinse it in the tub, then check the washers for cracks. A flattened washer will drip again in a month. Reinsert the trap with the beveled washer edge toward the fitting, tighten by hand, then a quarter-turn more.

Run the tap for a minute and wipe every joint with a dry finger. If it still gurgles after this, the clog is farther along. For that, call us about drain cleaning.

Older vanities

Pedestal sinks, wall-hung sinks and pre-war vanities

A pedestal sink hides the trap behind the column, and the supplies and drain usually run straight to the wall. You have less room to work, but fewer parts. Look for a chrome-plated brass trap, often heavier and older than plastic.

Wall-hung sinks sit on a bracket, and the bracket can loosen. If the basin shifts when you lean on it, stop. Do not use the sink until the bracket is secured, because the load stresses the drain and supply connections.

Pre-war vanities often hide older pipe behind them. The wall stub may be cast iron, galvanized steel or lead. Do not cut or force old lead or heavy galvanized pipe. That is licensed plumber work.

Brookline note

Older buildings, shared walls and who can do the work

The median Brookline housing unit was built in 1938, and about 76 percent of units are in buildings with two or more units (U.S. Census American Community Survey, 2020 to 2024 estimates). In a three-decker or a condo, your sink often shares a wall or a stack with a neighbor. A leak that runs behind the vanity can show up one floor down, so shut the stop first and call early.

Massachusetts says permits for plumbing work are issued only to licensed plumbers, and notes that faucet repair and clearing a blocked drain do not need a permit (per the state plumbers consumer fact sheet). Brookline's Building Department adds that homeowners cannot pull plumbing permits. In practice: tightening a nut and cleaning a trap are yours, replacing pipe in the wall is not.

MWRA reports its water as soft at about 1 grain per gallon (per MWRA's monthly report). Soft water leaves little scale on stops and pop-up parts, so the usual Brookline failures are old rubber, old washers and corroded metal rather than mineral buildup.
