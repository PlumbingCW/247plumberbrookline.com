Replacing a Water Heater Thermostat: A Brookline Guide

Electric tanks only. Gas and oil units have different controls and are not a homeowner repair.

By the Brookline 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 23, 2026 · Updated October 23, 2026

Call (617) 752-0573

To replace the thermostat on an electric water heater, switch off the breaker and prove the power is dead with a tester. Then remove the access panel and insulation, swap the old thermostat for a matching one, set it, and replace the cover before restoring power. A typical tank has two thermostats and two heating elements. Replace the one that failed, or both if the tank is old.

These steps are for electric tank heaters only. If you smell gas, leave the building and call 911 or National Grid at 1-800-233-5325 before you call us. For gas, oil or tankless units, or if any wire looks scorched, call (617) 752-0573 for water heater repair.

First, is it the thermostat?

Signs the thermostat is the problem

Elements, thermostats and the high-limit switch all produce similar complaints. Rule out the cheap items first.

  • ✓Lukewarm water. The lower thermostat or lower element is failing.
  • ✓Water too hot. A stuck thermostat that never shuts the element off. Treat as a scald risk.
  • ✓No hot water at all. Check the breaker and the red reset button on the upper thermostat before replacing anything.
  • ✓Reset button trips repeatedly. A shorted element or a bad upper thermostat.
  • ✓Hot water runs out fast. The lower element or lower thermostat is not working, or the tank is full of sediment.

Safety first

Power off, then prove it

Turn off the double-pole breaker for the water heater. Do not rely on the dial alone. Remove the upper access panel and use a non-contact voltage tester or a multimeter across the terminals. It must read zero before you touch any wire.

Water heaters run on 240 volts. That is enough to kill you. If you cannot confirm the power is off, if the panel is wet, or if the wiring looks burned, stop and call.

The job

Replacing an electric thermostat, step by step

Have a Phillips screwdriver, a flat-head, a multimeter or tester, and the replacement thermostat in hand. Match the part to the manufacturer and model on the tank label.

  1. Kill the power. Breaker off, then test at the terminals.
  2. Open the panel. Remove the screws and fold back the insulation. Take a photo of the wiring.
  3. Remove the plastic cover. A protector sits over the upper thermostat. Lift it off.
  4. Disconnect the wires. Loosen the screws or pull the connectors, one at a time. Label each.
  5. Pull the old thermostat. It clips against the tank. Slide it out.
  6. Press in the new one. It must sit flat against the steel for the sensor to read the tank. Gaps cause wrong readings.
  7. Reconnect and tighten. Match the photo. Wires on terminals should be snug, with no bare copper outside the screw.
  8. Set the dial. Many manufacturers set the thermostat around 120 degrees Fahrenheit. The state table for residential service lists 120 minimum and 130 maximum.
  9. Close it up. Replace the insulation, the cover and the panel. Make sure the tank is full of water before power comes on.
The dry-fire rule

Never power a tank that is not full

Electric elements burn out in minutes without water around them. After any repair, open a hot tap and let it run until water flows steadily with no air spitting. Only then restore the breaker.

Wait about an hour, then check the water temperature at a tap. Check again that evening. A properly installed thermostat should hold steady and the reset button should not trip.

Test it

Check the elements before blaming the thermostat

If the new thermostat does not fix it, the element is the next suspect.

What each test reading means

When to stop

Call a professional if any of these show up

Scorch marks, melted wire insulation, a tripped breaker that trips again, water leaking from the tank or a rusty bottom all point past a thermostat. A leaking tank cannot be repaired, and the answer is water heater replacement.

Tanks over about ten to twelve years old are often better replaced than repaired, because the next failure usually follows soon. Also call if the temperature and pressure relief valve discharges. The state plumbing code (248 CMR) says its discharge pipe must run full size with no shutoff to within twelve inches of the floor, and that piping is not a place for a shortcut. Brookline note

What Brookline owners should know before opening the panel

The Brookline Building Department says all work associated with wiring, plumbing or gas fitting must be performed by licensed individuals, and homeowners cannot pull plumbing or gas permits (per the Town's published FAQ). A like-for-like part swap on an existing appliance is maintenance, but if you are unsure that it counts, ask the Building Department before you start or call a licensed plumber.

Massachusetts 248 CMR 10.00 says a check valve must not be installed in the cold-water supply to a water heater, and that a closed system needs a properly sized expansion tank (per the state code). If your tank often weeps from the relief valve, the problem may be thermal expansion, not the thermostat.

Brookline tenants make up about 53 percent of occupied homes (U.S. Census American Community Survey, 2020 to 2024). If you rent, the heater is usually the landlord's, so tell them about a failing thermostat. State rules set 120 to 130 degrees as the residential range, which helps with scald safety. Mass Save also lists heat pump water heater rebates if the tank is near the end of its life.
