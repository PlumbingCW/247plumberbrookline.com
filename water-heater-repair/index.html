Symptoms

No hot water: what usually went wrong

A heater that goes cold overnight has a short list of likely causes, and the fuel type narrows it fast.

  • ✓Gas, no flame. A pilot that blew out, a dirty thermocouple or a failed igniter on an electronic-ignition unit.
  • ✓Electric, lukewarm or cold. A burned-out heating element or a tripped high-limit switch.
  • ✓Hot at first, then cold. A cracked dip tube sends cold water to the outlet and mixes it with the hot.
  • ✓Rumbling, popping or whining. Sediment on the bottom of the tank is boiling under the burner or element.
  • ✓A flashing code on the control. Newer units report faults, and the code points to the part to test.

What we check

How we find the failed part

  1. Look and listen. We check the tank, fittings and pan for moisture, then the flue, burner and wiring for scorch marks or loose connections.
  2. Test with a meter. A multimeter checks elements, thermostats and the high-limit switch on electric units, and the thermocouple output on gas units.
  3. Check temperature and pressure. A thermometer at a hot tap and a pressure gauge on a hose bib show whether the setting and the house pressure are in range.
  4. Judge the tank. We tap the shell, look at the age on the data plate and inspect the drain-down water for rust, so you know if a repair is worth paying for.

Massachusetts code lists 120 degrees Fahrenheit as the minimum and 130 as the maximum for residential hot water, so we adjust thermostats inside that range.

Safety valve

The relief valve that keeps dripping

Every tank carries a temperature and pressure relief valve, and 248 CMR requires its discharge pipe to be full size, with no shut-off, ending within twelve inches of the floor. A valve that opens occasionally is doing its job, and a valve that drips all the time has either worn out or is reacting to excess pressure.

A check valve is not allowed on the cold supply to a heater, but a meter or backflow device elsewhere in the house can create the same closed system. In that case the code calls for a properly sized thermal expansion tank, and adding one often ends the drip. Never cap the discharge pipe, because that disables the safety device.

If your heater is working and the valve is simply weeping, our water heater installation page explains how we size and place expansion tanks on new work.

Worth knowing

Anode rods, sediment and soft water

The anode rod is a sacrificial metal stick that corrodes so the tank does not. Swapping a spent rod on a tank that is otherwise sound can add years, and we will tell you if the tank is already too far gone for the rod to matter.

Brookline's water is soft, about 1 grain per gallon in MWRA's monthly report, so scale is slower to build than in hard-water towns. Sediment still accumulates, and a periodic flush through the drain valve removes it.

Before we arrive

Leak or no heat at the heater: what to do first

Shut the cold supply

If water is escaping from the tank or a fitting, close the valve on the cold pipe above the heater.

Do not relight a stuck pilot

If the unit has locked out more than once, repeated relighting only wastes time. Note any code on the display for the technician.

Gas smell means leave

If you smell gas, leave the building and call 911 or National Grid at 1-800-233-5325 before calling us.

Dry the area

Mop standing water and move stored items off the floor, especially cardboard, so we can see the source of the leak.

Know before you call

What drives the cost of a heater repair

  • The failed partAn igniter, a thermostat and an element cost different amounts, and a valve differs from a dip tube.
  • Age of the tankPutting a new part into a 15 year old tank is a different decision than fixing a 5 year old one.
  • Fuel typeGas work involves the burner assembly and venting checks, while electric work centers on the elements and wiring.
  • Time of dayA call in the middle of the night is different from a same-day daytime visit.
  • AccessA heater tucked behind a boiler or in a tight closet takes longer to reach.

Repair costs for wear and tear are normally not covered by a homeowner policy. Water damage from a sudden leak sometimes is, so keep photos.
