Sump Pump Battery Backup Installation in Brookline, MA

The pump only fails when the storm is worst. Here is how a backup works, which type fits a Brookline basement, and what the job involves.

By the Brookline 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 23, 2026 · Updated October 23, 2026

Call (617) 752-0573

A sump pump battery backup is a second pump, a deep-cycle battery, a charger and a controller that take over when house power fails or the main pump quits. It runs from a battery for several hours of pumping, depending on battery size and how fast water is coming in. The usual setup is a smaller backup pump mounted in the same pit, set a few inches higher than the main one.

Nor'easters and summer thunderstorms often take out power in the same hours that the ground is saturated. That is the exact moment an electric-only pump stops while the pit fills. If your basement has ever shown water at the floor edge, a backup is cheap protection compared with the stuff stored down there. Our sump pump repair work starts with the same question: what happens when the power goes out?

Who needs one

Signs your pit needs a second line of defense

Not every basement needs a backup. These are the situations where we recommend one.

  • ✓The pump runs often in storms. If it cycles every minute or two during heavy rain, the pit has little reserve time once power drops.
  • ✓You finished the basement. Flooring, drywall and a furnace or water heater on the slab raise the cost of a single failure.
  • ✓Outages are common on your street. Overhead lines and tree limbs mean long outages after wind storms.
  • ✓The main pump is old. A pump past ten years, with a stuck float or a humming motor, can fail with or without a blackout.
  • ✓You are away often. A backup with an alarm only helps if someone hears it, so travel changes the plan.

Your options

Battery backup versus water-powered backup

There are two common types. They solve the same problem in very different ways, and the right pick depends on your basement and your water supply.

Backup pump types compared
FeatureBattery backupWater-powered backup
Power source12-volt deep-cycle battery with a chargerWater pressure from the house supply line
RuntimeHours, set by battery size and inflowNo time limit while supply pressure holds
UpkeepTest monthly, replace the battery every few yearsTest monthly, check the valve and fittings
Water useNoneUses drinking water while it runs, so a long storm can raise the water bill
Biggest riskDead or neglected batteryLow supply pressure, and local rules on backflow protection
Best fitMost homes with a clean pit and an outlet nearbyHomes with strong supply pressure and no wish to maintain a battery

Water-powered units are not accepted everywhere. Ask the Building Department and your plumber before choosing one.

In practice we lean toward a battery system for most basements. It does not depend on the supply line, and the upkeep is one simple test. If the outage lasts longer than the battery, a generator or a larger battery bank is the next step, and we can talk that through on the phone. If water also seeps through the walls, a pump is only part of the answer, and basement waterproofing is the other half.

The job

What a battery backup installation involves

Most installs take part of a day when the pit is in good shape. The steps below are what a plumber does and what you can expect to see.

  1. Inspect the pit. We check the liner, depth, debris, and the main pump and float. A pit full of silt gets cleaned first, because a backup pump sitting in grit fails early.
  2. Size the backup. The backup pump should move enough gallons per minute to keep up with your worst storm, not just match the old pump on a dry day.
  3. Set the second float. The backup float sits a few inches above the main float so it only starts when the main pump has failed or cannot keep up.
  4. Run a separate discharge or tie in correctly. A backup needs its own check valve so water pumped by one pump does not fall back through the other.
  5. Mount the battery and controller. The battery lives in a ventilated box off the floor, with the charger on a working outlet. A dedicated, protected circuit is best, and wiring changes should go to a licensed electrician.
  6. Test with water. We unplug the main pump, fill the pit and watch the backup start, move water and shut off. Then we show you the alarm and the monthly test.
Where the water goes

Discharge: what is allowed and what is not

The backup should send water to the same place as the main pump, usually outside and away from the foundation. It should never be tied into a sanitary drain or toilet line.

Brookline's sewer overflow guidance says that downspouts and sump pumps do not discharge to sewer drains. Clear, rain-driven groundwater in the sewer is one of the reasons the town runs an inflow and infiltration program. If your current pump drains into a floor drain or a sink, tell us before we touch it.

Know your pump

A sump pump is not a sewage ejector

Some basements have a different pit that collects wastewater from a basement bathroom or laundry. That one is a sewage ejector, and it follows different rules. Under 248 CMR 10.15(9), discharge piping from all sewage pumps and ejectors must contain an accessible backwater or check valve and an accessible full port shut off valve.

The same section sets a minimum of 20 gallons per minute for a single-family ejector pump. A backup for an ejector pit is a separate design from a clear water sump backup, so say which pit you have when you call. If you are not sure, the lid and the incoming pipes usually tell us on the first visit.

Do it yourself or call

What a homeowner can do and where to stop

You can test the system, vacuum debris from the pit with the power off, check that the discharge line outside is clear of leaves and ice, and clean battery terminals. Those jobs are safe and worth doing each season.

Stop and call us for anything that changes the plumbing: a new check valve, a new discharge route, a swapped pump or a pit that needs cutting. Stop and call an electrician for new outlets or wiring. Never reach into a pit with a pump plugged in, and never work in standing water with power on.

  • Monthly: . pour a bucket of water into the pit, watch both pumps start and the water drop.
  • Twice a year: . inspect the battery, tighten terminals and check the charger light.
  • Every few years: . replace the battery. A battery that tests fine in September may fail in March.
Know before you call

What moves the price of a backup system

Cost depends on the parts and the pit, not the day of the week. Here is what changes the number.

  • Battery sizeA larger deep-cycle battery pumps for more hours and costs more than a compact one.
  • Backup pump ratingHigher gallons-per-minute pumps cost more and suit pits that fill quickly in storms.
  • Pit conditionA cracked liner, deep silt or a pit that is too small for two pumps adds labor.
  • Discharge workA shared line needs a second check valve, and a new route outdoors takes more pipe and time.
  • Alarm and monitoringA basic buzzer is simple. Phone-connected alarms add parts and setup.

Some homeowner policies cover water backup only with an added endorsement, and many exclude groundwater seepage. Check your policy before a storm, not after.
